🧠 Emergent Behaviour

🚦
Spontaneous Queue Formation
No robot was programmed to queue. Yet when 30 robots all independently choose the nearest shelf, they converge on the same aisles and a bottleneck emerges from purely local rules. Each robot only knows about its immediate surroundings — the global pattern is an emergent property.
"Global order arising from local interactions — the hallmark of emergence."
🔋
Charging Cycle Desynchronisation
Initially all robots start with full batteries and drain simultaneously, causing a wave of charging requests at the same ticks. Over time, collisions and path lengths differ per robot, spreading battery levels apart. By tick 300+ the charging queue shrinks naturally — the system self-organises.
"Desynchronisation is a second-order emergent effect of heterogeneous path lengths."
🔥
Congestion Hot-Spots
The heatmap reveals persistent hot corridors between shelves and packing stations. No robot plans a route — each follows the greedy nearest-target rule. Collectively they wear down the same paths, creating a traffic pattern similar to ant pheromone trails.
"Stigmergy without pheromones: congestion heat decays at 5%/tick, reflecting recent traffic."
📦
Shelf Depletion Waves
With many robots, the nearest shelves deplete rapidly, forcing robots to travel further. This creates a natural load-balancing wave where distant shelves become the primary source, and robots spread across the warehouse — an emergent spatial distribution with no central coordinator.
"Greedy local selection produces globally distributed foraging."
⚡
Diminishing Returns (Scaling)
Doubling robots from 5→15 significantly increases throughput. But 15→30 produces a smaller gain. Congestion, obstacle collisions, and charger contention create negative feedback. The system self-limits — an emergent upper bound on throughput given fixed infrastructure.
"Infrastructure bottlenecks cap the benefit of adding more agents."
🔄
Decentralised Coordination
There is no central dispatcher. Robots share only the environment (patches): a robot picking up an item decrements the shelf count, which other robots sense implicitly when choosing targets. This passive information sharing — reading the environment — is a form of stigmergic coordination.
"The warehouse grid is the shared memory — coordination without communication."

Section 13 — Emergent Behaviour Analysis

The warehouse robot simulation demonstrates several emergent behaviours that arise solely from the interaction of simple local rules. No robot has global knowledge, a communication channel, or explicit coordination logic.

Key finding: Emergent queuing forms at shelf aisles when robot count exceeds ~10. This is a direct consequence of the greedy nearest-shelf selection rule operating simultaneously across multiple agents. Each robot acts rationally from its own perspective; the queue is a collective artefact.

In Scenario 1 (5 robots, 5 obstacles) the system operates below capacity. Robots rarely interact and throughput scales linearly with robot count. Battery cycles are mostly synchronised because all robots deplete at similar rates with similar path lengths.

In Scenario 2 (15 robots, 15 obstacles) moderate congestion appears around the left-side shelf zone. The heatmap shows a hot corridor forming. Collision avoidance adds latency — robots turn away from each other, sometimes heading away from their target, increasing effective path length and battery consumption.

In Scenario 3 (30 robots, 20 obstacles) two distinct emergent phenomena are observable: (1) charging queue surges in early ticks when all robots deplete simultaneously, then gradual desynchronisation as battery levels diverge; and (2) shelf depletion cascades where the nearest shelves empty so rapidly that robots are forced into longer cross-warehouse journeys, naturally spreading traffic.

These behaviours were not programmed — they emerged from the interaction of battery drain, greedy target selection, and collision avoidance. This is the defining characteristic of a multi-agent emergent system.
